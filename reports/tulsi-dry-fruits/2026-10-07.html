Competitor Intel · Day 6

Tulsi Dry Fruits

Researched October 7, 2026

Origin story

Founded by Balbeer Bajaj in Amritsar, Punjab. Accounts of the exact start date differ slightly across sources — one profile (2024) traces the business to the 1950s, importing and marketing Afghan dry fruits as a trading venture; a 2019 YourStory profile of Bajaj (then 74) places his own entry into the business in the mid-1960s, as the family's sole breadwinner after losing his father at age 10. Either way this is a six-decade-old trading house, not a startup.

That original venture, KBB Nuts, stayed a pure trading business for nearly three decades and plateaued around ₹2–3 crore turnover. Bajaj's own conclusion was that trading alone had a ceiling — real scale required owning manufacturing. In 1996 he opened a dry-fruit processing unit in Amritsar and launched the consumer-facing Tulsi brand on top of the trading business. In 2013, the group added Gourmia as a separate, deliberately premium/gourmet brand sitting above Tulsi's mass-market positioning.

The whole operation now sits under the umbrella Bajaj Food Group (legal entity KBB Nuts Pvt Ltd / SKB International), which frames itself as having "entire control on the product logistics from the origin" — i.e. the moat is backward integration (own de-shelling, processing, cold storage) rather than a D2C brand story.

Where they sell — the full picture

D2C sites: tulsidryfruits.com (retail + a separate "Bulk Buy" wholesale portal + an Exports page) and gourmiaindia.com (separate storefront for the premium line) — both have working cart/checkout.

Marketplaces: Amazon, Flipkart, Meesho, JioMart (per Gourmia's own "where to buy" list).

Quick commerce: No Blinkit/Zepto/Instamart presence found in any source — a notable gap for a ₹700 Cr+ player in 2026.

Modern trade / hypermarkets: Metro Cash & Carry, Spar Hypermarket, Tesco, Spencer's — i.e. organised retail and B2B cash-and-carry, not just general trade.

General trade: Pan-India distribution reaching 500+ cities for the Tulsi brand specifically.

Institutional/B2B: A distinct, large channel most online-first competitors don't have — supplies Army and Police canteens, Indian Railways, and airports, plus raw-ingredient supply to ice-cream, biscuit, namkeen and Ayurveda manufacturers. Minimum institutional order is 200 kg, with smaller trial dispatch available.

Manufacturing footprint: Processing facilities in Delhi, Punjab (Amritsar — including a dedicated cashew plant), Haryana and Andhra Pradesh; FSSC 22000 and HACCP certified.

Exports: Reported (2019) as reaching 20+ countries across the Americas, Europe and the Middle East.

Strategy, positioning & product range

Tulsi positions itself as "India's largest edible nuts and dry fruits brand" with the widest raw-and-flavoured range — a volume and distribution-breadth claim, not a premium/craft one. The company's own language leans entirely on supply-chain control and mechanised hygiene ("our strength is our supply chain; we have entire control on the product logistics from the origin") rather than on brand storytelling, founder narrative, or lifestyle marketing.

Gourmia, launched specifically to capture quality-focused, higher-spend buyers, is the brand's answer to modern D2C/gourmet positioning — flavoured and exotic SKUs (jalapeño, sour cream & onion, Thai chilli cashews, macadamias, pecans), sold through its own site and organised retail rather than general trade.

Overall this is a diversified, multi-brand, multi-channel FMCG house built on six decades of backward-integrated sourcing and processing — the opposite architecture from venture-backed, content-led D2C challengers like Farmley or Happilo. Its growth engine is distribution depth (500+ cities, institutional contracts, export markets) rather than digital acquisition or brand.

What VUTAS can take from this

  • Don't compete with Tulsi where it's strong. Its moat is backward-integrated processing, cold storage and 500-city general-trade distribution built over 60 years — none of that is replicable or even relevant to VUTAS's near-term plan. VUTAS's edge has to stay in curation, brand, and the online experience, not supply-chain cost leadership.
  • The Tulsi/Gourmia split is a template, but VUTAS should pick one lane fully. Bajaj Food Group runs mass (Tulsi) and premium (Gourmia) as separate brands precisely because one brand can't credibly hold both positions. VUTAS, being a single curated under-20-SKU brand, should lean all the way into the Gourmia-like premium/gourmet position (closer to its Alpine Citrus direction) rather than trying to also chase mass-market price points — that fight belongs to Tulsi, Nutraj and the Amazon/quick-commerce value players.
  • A ₹700 Cr+ legacy player still has no quick-commerce presence and a thin digital brand story. That's a real, exploitable gap: a modern Next.js site with fast checkout, strong visual branding and an actual founder/story narrative is something Tulsi conspicuously lacks. VUTAS's ongoing site rebuild is directly competing in a space this incumbent has left open.
  • Pricing anchor check: Tulsi's own-site pricing (₹375–₹415 for 200 g premium walnuts/pistachios, ~₹1,449/kg for almonds) and Gourmia's higher band (₹345–₹845 for similarly sized packs) give VUTAS two real reference points from an old-guard operator — useful for sanity-checking where VUTAS's own pricing should sit relative to "mass legacy" vs. "premium legacy," not just newer D2C brands.
  • Institutional/bulk B2B (canteens, Railways, FMCG ingredient supply) is a channel VUTAS hasn't touched — not urgent today, but worth filing away as a possible future revenue line once the D2C base and brand are established, the way Tulsi's institutional arm now coexists with its consumer brands.
  • Two slightly conflicting founding-date accounts across sources (1950s vs mid-1960s) is a reminder for VUTAS's own "About" story on the new site: keep the founding narrative simple, consistent, and stated once clearly, rather than letting press repeat varying versions over time.
