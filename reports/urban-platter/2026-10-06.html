Competitor Intel · Day 5

Urban Platter

Researched October 6, 2026

Origin story

Founded in 2015 in Mumbai by Chirag Kenia (Founder & Managing Partner) and Dhaval Kenia (Co-Founder). Chirag brought over a decade of e-commerce and marketing experience and started not by building a branded nut company but by reselling gourmet products on eBay India and Amazon India — a classic marketplace-arbitrage entry rather than a branded-D2C-from-day-one play.

No external funding was involved at founding — the business was bootstrapped, funded from the founders' own capital and reinvested marketplace revenue. Their market read: India's urbanizing middle class was developing international, gourmet food tastes (olive oils, nut butters, specialty spices, baking ingredients, vegan products) faster than any single Indian brand was stocking them, and marketplaces (not yet a crowded D2C space in 2015) were the cheapest way to test demand across categories before committing to inventory or manufacturing.

The company grew from a reseller into a house-brand manufacturer: 2018 reporting already showed 2,800+ SKUs listed, 1,000 of them in-house branded, a 37-person Mumbai team, in-house cold storage, and ~₹2 crore/month revenue off roughly 36,000 monthly orders — a breadth-first strategy from very early on, the opposite of a curated catalog.

Funding: No institutional funding round was found anywhere in press, Tracxn-style financial trackers, or founder interviews — Urban Platter remains fully bootstrapped a decade in. No valuation exists publicly because there's no priced round.

Growth has decelerated sharply. A founder interview (post-2022, referencing the pandemic retrospectively) cited 40–50% YoY growth "over the past two years" and a 180-person team — but the most recent filed financials (FY24) show growth collapsing to 8.7% while total expenses grew 16% and total assets fell 9%, producing a widening loss. Ten years in, at ~₹54 Cr revenue, the business is still unprofitable — a cautionary data point on scaling via catalog breadth (2,800+ SKUs) without matching margin discipline.

Where they sell

Started and still anchored on marketplaces: Amazon India has historically carried the large majority of revenue (~90% per the 2018 YourStory account; a later founder interview puts total online at 80% with Amazon as "the primary revenue platform," implying marketplaces still dominate over the owned site). Products are also listed on BigBasket and 1mg (notable for a food brand — likely wellness/health-food crossover positioning for items like almond milk, seeds, nut butters).

Owned D2C: urbanplatter.in — contributed only ~5% of revenue as of the 2018 data point; no evidence found that this ratio has meaningfully shifted since (site pricing itself was hard to crawl cleanly during this research, which is itself telling — see takeaways).

Quick commerce: No confirmed Blinkit/Zepto/Instamart listings found in this research pass — if accurate, this is a real gap for a Mumbai-based gourmet brand given how fast instant-delivery grocery has grown; worth re-checking in a future pass.

Offline: First (and so far, only confirmed) flagship retail store opened in Bandra, Mumbai in December 2021 — an experiential, tasting-led format stocking ~900-1,000 products, deliberately placed where founder Chirag Kenia said "a large chunk of the target audience resides in or shops." A 2022–23 interview mentioned plans for 30 stores pan-India within 12 months, but no subsequent press confirms that rollout happened at scale — worth treating as an unrealized plan, not a confirmed footprint. Delivery is claimed to reach "100% pin codes" / 700+ locations across India via courier/logistics partners, plus a corporate-gifting channel.

Strategy, positioning & product range

Urban Platter is not a dry-fruit-snacking brand in the Happilo/Nutraj sense — it's a general gourmet-and-specialty-ingredients grocer that happens to sell nuts and dry fruits as one of many categories (alongside olive oils, pasta, spice blends, baking ingredients, vegan milks, seeds, curry pastes). Mission, per founder Chirag Kenia: "make high-quality global ingredients accessible to Indian consumers via digital channels."

The strategy is explicitly digital-first by operational necessity, not just preference — Kenia: "When you have such a wide variety of products, it's almost impossible to execute a business offline." With 2,800+ SKUs (1,200 active at one point, 1,000+ in-house brand) sourced from 300+ vendors through a 1,200 sq ft repacking facility, the whole model depends on e-commerce logistics and automation to stay viable — breadth is the moat, not curation.

Notably, the brand's best-seller is Pink Himalayan Rock Salt — not a nut or dry fruit at all — illustrating how a maximal-breadth catalog finds its anchor products almost accidentally rather than by design. Repeat-purchase ratio is a healthy 40–45%, suggesting real retention once a customer becomes a regular gourmet-grocery shopper, even if the catalog is sprawling.

What VUTAS can take from this

  • Catalog breadth is not a free win — treat Urban Platter's deceleration as a warning, not a model. A decade in, with 2,800+ SKUs and ₹54 Cr revenue, Urban Platter is still posting a net loss and its growth has collapsed from 40–50% to under 9% YoY while expenses keep climbing. That's strong real-world evidence in favor of VUTAS's own under-20-SKU discipline — don't read "more products = more revenue" as a safe assumption; breadth without margin discipline is how a brand ends up big and unprofitable.
  • Marketplace dependency is a trap that's hard to escape even after 10 years. Urban Platter still gets the bulk of its revenue through Amazon, with its own site stuck around ~5%. Since VUTAS is deliberately investing in a real Next.js/Vercel D2C rebuild with full checkout, use that investment to actively build owned-channel demand (email, WhatsApp, retargeting, subscribe-and-save) from day one rather than treating the new site as "just another sales channel" alongside marketplaces — the goal should be to flip that ratio in VUTAS's favor compared to where Urban Platter is stuck.
  • Basic e-commerce execution is a real differentiator, not a given. Urban Platter's own site was difficult to even crawl cleanly for this report (broken category pages, empty content) despite ₹54 Cr in revenue. That's a low bar VUTAS's new Next.js build can clear decisively: fast, indexable, reliably-priced product pages are a genuine competitive edge against a bigger but technically weaker incumbent.
  • Identify VUTAS's own "accidental hero SKU" early. Urban Platter's best-seller (Pink Himalayan Rock Salt) isn't even a nut — proof that in any catalog, however small, one or two items end up carrying disproportionate repeat demand. VUTAS should actively track per-SKU repeat-purchase and conversion data from the new site's launch so marketing spend can concentrate on whichever 1–2 products (likely almonds, cashews, or anjeer) are actually pulling customers back, instead of spreading effort evenly across the catalog.
  • Experiential retail is a late-game move, not a launch strategy. Urban Platter only opened its first physical store in 2021 — six years after founding, once the digital brand and repeat-customer base were established. This validates VUTAS's current sequencing: get the D2C site and checkout right first; physical/experiential presence, if ever, comes much later.
  • Watch import-cost exposure on non-Indian-sourced nuts. Urban Platter's pecan pricing swung from ₹580 to ₹1,649 over two years — a reminder that if VUTAS carries any imported nuts (pecans, Californian almonds/pistachios/walnuts), pricing needs buffer room or more frequent review versus domestically-sourced items like cashews, raisins, or anjeer.
