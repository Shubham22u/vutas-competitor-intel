Competitor Intel · Day 7

Nutty Gritties

Researched October 8, 2026

Origin story

Founded by Dinika Bhatia, born in Delhi in 1987 and raised in Mumbai, into a family that has traded nuts and dry fruits for six generations since 1887. Her father, Raju Bhatia, runs California Agri Nuts Corporation — the family's sourcing business — and became her first backer.

The idea came early: she first conceived a flavoured-nuts snack brand at 15 after noticing how differently people snacked abroad, and tested it at 16 by running a stall at a Rakhi mela in Delhi. She formalised it as her final business-plan thesis at the University of Southern California in 2009 (winning USC's Best Business Idea Award), picking up hands-on experience at an almond brokerage and a pistachio factory in California before returning home.

She moved back to India in June 2009, incorporated in August, and launched the brand under the family entity DRB Foods in September 2009 — starting with just ₹50 lakh from her father and a team of three (now ~120 people). Her read on the market: Indian dry fruits were a ~95%-unorganised wholesale commodity business, consumed mainly around winter, Diwali and weddings, with no everyday "healthy snacking" format and no retail (as opposed to wholesale) branded nut player at all — a gap she set out to fill as, by several accounts, India's first packaged flavoured-nuts brand.

Early going was hard: shifting a price-driven, commodity-minded trade to branded retail; absorbing currency volatility on imported raw material (later hedged); building out a factory in Okhla, Delhi; and personally pitching the brand to retailers and shoppers. First sales came through a single Modern Bazaar store in Vasant Vihar, Delhi in 2009; by 2010 she'd landed Walmart and Barista as clients. The brand adopted a D2C model and launched its own website in 2015, and COVID-era lockdowns pushed e-commerce demand up 5x — a gain that held after restrictions lifted.

Funding: fully bootstrapped. No public source (Crunchbase, Tracxn, YourStory, trade press) reports any institutional VC round, angel round beyond the founder's father, or disclosed valuation — the company has self-funded its growth from ₹50 lakh in 2009 to a company it says has been profitable for the last several years and is "nearing ₹100 crore" in revenue. It's now investing a fresh ₹20 Cr (~$2.1M) in a second manufacturing facility alongside the Okhla plant, aiming for a 6x capacity jump and a stated long-run capacity target of ₹600 Cr/year — funded internally, not through a raise. Publicly stated ambition is 5x revenue growth from FY25 to FY27, and eventually a "billion-dollar company."

Where they sell

D2C: nuttygritties.com  ·  Marketplaces: Amazon (part of the Amazon Launchpad program for homegrown brands), Flipkart, BigBasket  ·  Quick commerce: Blinkit (reportedly bursts of ~10,000 units/day) and Zepto.

Modern retail: Nature's Basket, Food Hall, TwentyFour Seven, Namdhari's, More and Future Group stores. Cash & carry: Metro, Walmart. Beyond retail: airlines, airports, railways, vending machines, cafes (Barista), plus corporate festive snacking, corporate gifting and wedding gifting as a standing B2B2C channel.

Footprint: as of 2026, reported presence in 8,000+ premium stores, supermarkets, cafes, vending machines and airports across 100+ cities — up from roughly "2,000 stores in 13 (or by one account 27) cities" in earlier press, showing rapid offline expansion alongside online growth. Revenue is described as split roughly evenly across D2C, marketplaces/e-commerce and physical retail. International expansion is stated as an active exploration ("markets where demand for functional, health-focused foods is growing") but no specific countries or numbers are public yet.

Strategy & positioning

Nutty Gritties frames itself as the brand that "modernised the traditional nut" — turning a commodity bought by weight into a flavoured, branded, grab-and-go snack. It claims a string of category firsts in India: first retail (not wholesale) nut brand, first to introduce flavoured nuts like chilli-garlic cashews, and an early mover in popularising trail mixes domestically.

Dinika Bhatia has described the channel strategy explicitly: "We've used retail stores as a new customer acquisition platform and online as a customer retention platform." That is, physical shelf presence (airports, cafes, modern retail, vending machines) exists mainly to get discovered; the website and e-commerce relationship is where she expects repeat purchase and margin. On what's missing from Indian food brands generally, she's said: "What we often lack is stronger storytelling, innovation, and premium positioning."

The brand layers a CSR/purpose narrative — "Gifting with Purpose" — on top of the product: supporting women entrepreneurs and artisans (about 70% of its own workforce is women), animal welfare programs, and child-nutrition funding via Spread A Smile India, alongside work with farmers. Quick commerce is explicitly positioned as a Gen Z, impulse-purchase, lower-CAC channel, run in parallel with the gifting/airline/corporate channel at the other end of the spectrum.

What VUTAS can take from this

  • Bootstrapped-to-near-₹100Cr is proof the category doesn't require VC — but it took 16 years. Nutty Gritties went from ₹50 lakh family capital (2009) to nearing ₹100 Cr (2026) entirely self-funded, reinvesting profit into capacity. That's encouraging for VUTAS's no-VC posture, but it's also a realistic timeline check — don't model a 3-5 year path to that scale off this comp; model a decade-plus one, funded by margin discipline, not a sprint.
  • "Retail as acquisition, online as retention" is a channel principle VUTAS can borrow without owning a single store. VUTAS doesn't need physical shelf space to copy the logic: treat Amazon/Flipkart/quick-commerce listings as the low-cost discovery layer, and the new Next.js D2C site (vutas.in) as the place built for repeat purchase — better margins, cleaner UX, saved payment details, and eventually subscriptions/reorder nudges that a marketplace listing can't offer.
  • SKU proliferation (45+ products, 100+ flavours, new launches every quarter) is the opposite of VUTAS's curated under-20-SKU bet — treat that contrast as a positioning asset, not a gap to close. Rather than feeling pressure to chase catalog breadth to compete, VUTAS can message directly against it: "we didn't launch 100 flavours — we picked the dozen that are worth your money," which is a sharper, more defensible story for a small curated brand than trying to out-SKU a 16-year-old incumbent.
  • Quick commerce demand for flavoured nuts is real and large (bursts of ~10,000 units/day on Blinkit alone) — worth testing 2-3 VUTAS hero SKUs there even before the new site fully launches. It's close to zero-CAC discovery for impulse snacking in exactly VUTAS's category, and testing it now generates sales-velocity data VUTAS can use to prioritise which SKUs deserve hero placement on the new site.
  • Corporate/festive/wedding gifting is a high-margin B2B2C channel that's easy to deprioritise while focused on D2C rebuild — don't skip it this Diwali season. Nutty Gritties runs this alongside airlines and vending machines as a standing revenue line, not an afterthought; a simple "Corporate & Festive Gifting" page on the new VUTAS site, timed to the current festive calendar, is low-effort relative to the D2C rebuild and taps a buyer who cares more about presentation and reliability than price.
  • Their ~10% standing discount (smaller than Happilo's ~15%) is a useful data point for VUTAS's own MRP/sale-price display on the new Next.js checkout — it suggests a modest, consistent discount reads as "fair everyday pricing" in this category, while VUTAS doesn't need to match a deep-discount optics game to look credible next to bigger players.
